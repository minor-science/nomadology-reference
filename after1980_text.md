When Deleuze and Guattari wrote the plateaus, they worked from the anthropology and archaeology of the 1970s. Those fields have not stood still in the decades since. Some of their findings bear out nomadology's claims. Others complicate them, and on a few points they dispute the plateaus outright. What pattern emerges? On the whole, the plateaus are most convincing when we take them as a theory of operations (the forming of a stock or the grading of a flight). Yet they are at their weakest when they make claims about origins or about which came first, which is why each row ends on what the record asks of a new nomadology.

After stage theory fell out of favor, no whole school in these fields has worked the way nomadology does. Rather, several smaller areas of research have done so in part, and it is these that we place here. For each area, the table gives what it studies (which of the five processes it examines, capture for instance) along with its way of working, as some areas follow their material while others impose a model on it. Its relation to nomadology then appears on a single scale, from nearest to farthest, with each judgment made about a work rather than about its author.

Nomadology is not confined to the fields it engages most often, which here are anthropology and archaeology, with history beside them. In their notes, Deleuze and Guattari also draw on mathematics, military theory, political economy and the history of science (Archimedes, Clausewitz, Marx, Husserl), and so must a new nomadology.
