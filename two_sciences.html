Nomadology's two sciences

Working out the thrust of the stone on site rather than from an architect's plan on paper, the Gothic journeymen who raised the cathedrals practiced what Deleuze and Guattari call a minor or nomad science (364-68). Such a science follows its material and poses problems that it cannot solve on its own (how to vault a nave higher than the Romanesque, for instance). As a result, its findings tend to be taken over by the royal science of the State, which reproduces, deduces, and extracts constants. But the two are not simply opposed, as they share "a single field of interaction" (367) in which what one appropriates, the other is forever cutting loose again. And it is this borderline that the middle column records, whether the passage is by translation, appropriation, repression, or the cutting loose of what was taken. Altogether, the sheet serves as a companion to The five machinic processes, which gives the whole matter a single row ("How is knowledge made?").

Working with another field

A nomadology can thus work with anthropology, archaeology, and history without taking on their frame along with their findings. Many of the questions these fields ask, such as how the State originated or who lived free of it, are asked from the side of the State, and each row below turns one of them into a question that a nomadology can pursue, with the pages that ground it and a case from the specialist literature.

Who and what is named

For readers who do not know the literature: each scholar and place on the sheet, in a line.

Archimedes, Democritus, Lucretius
Deleuze and Guattari take these as the ancient sources of minor science, namely Archimedes' geometry of curves and the atomism of Democritus and Lucretius, as Michel Serres treats them (361-62).
Bergson, Henri
Deleuze and Guattari find the relation between the two sciences "somewhat like intuition and intelligence in Bergson," where only intelligence can solve formally the problems that intuition poses (374).
Bernard de Clairvaux, Garin de Troyes
Bernard of Clairvaux, abbot in the twelfth century, together with Garin, by legend his monk-mason, whose "operative logic of movement" stands for the geometry of the Gothic journeymen (364).
Bilby, Kenneth
For the anthropologist Kenneth Bilby, in True-Born Maroons (2005), the Jamaican Maroons keep a knowledge they call “Maroon Science,” learned through “testing and evasion.”
Breiman, Leo
In "Statistical Modeling: The Two Cultures" (2001), the statistician Leo Breiman contrasts models of how data are generated with algorithms that predict without such a model.
Carney, Judith, and Richard Rosomoff
In In the Shadow of Slavery (2009), the geographers Judith Carney and Richard Rosomoff reproduce a 1769 militia map of a runaway settlement in Minas Gerais, Brazil, marking its smithies, gardens, hidden defenses, and “holes for escape” (85).
Casimir, Jean
The Haitian historian Jean Casimir shows in The Haitians (2020) how cultivators after the revolution built a “counter-plantation” of family farms and, “without moving from the plantations,” stopped being the plantation’s workers (142), a book Protevi also cites (RoV 104).
Desargues, Monge, Poncelet, Carnot, Vauban, Perronet
Engineers and mathematicians whom Deleuze and Guattari place on the borderline, from Vauban's fortifications and Desargues's perspective to Perronet's bridges (363-65).
Frangipane, Marcella
Marcella Frangipane, an archaeologist, describes the clay sealings of the "burnt village" at Sabi Abyad in northern Syria, some eight thousand years old, as records of stores shared "probably along egalitarian lines" (2018).
Green, Adam
In “Killing the Priest-King” (2021), the archaeologist Adam Green describes Indus cities (about 2600-1900 BCE) with writing, standard weights, seals, and metalwork, yet no palaces or royal tombs.
Hudson, Michael
Michael Hudson, an economic historian, reconstructs the royal cancellations of agrarian debt in the ancient Near East, the "clean slates" (2002).
Humphrey, Caroline, and David Sneath
Writing on Mongolia and Inner Asia in The End of Nomadism? (1999), the anthropologists Caroline Humphrey and David Sneath call nomadism “a category imagined by outsiders” and prefer “mobile pastoralism” (1).
Husserl, Edmund
Nomad science takes its anexact rigor from Edmund Husserl’s “vague essences,” roundness as opposed to the circle (367).
Indus, Sumer, Uruk
The Indus civilization flourished in present-day Pakistan and northwest India (about 2600-1900 BCE). Sumer names the city-states of southern Mesopotamia (from about 2900 BCE), while the period of Uruk, a Mesopotamian city (about 4000-3100 BCE), gives the earliest writing, kept as accounts.
Ingold, Tim
For the anthropologist Tim Ingold, setting food aside is not yet a stock, since a stock is rights converging “upon a single interest” (1983, 553).
Lévi-Strauss, Claude
Claude Lévi-Strauss showed that the same village can present itself as segmentary and egalitarian or as encompassing and hierarchized, two potentials that Deleuze and Guattari treat as anticipations of town and State (433).
Liebenberg, Louis, and the Shadow Wolves
In The Art of Tracking: The Origin of Science (1990), Louis Liebenberg studies Kalahari trackers. Today, the Shadow Wolves are a unit of Native American trackers working for United States immigration enforcement on the Tohono O'odham Nation in Arizona.
Moreno García, Juan Carlos, and Seth Richardson
In Monarchies and the Organization of Power (2025), the historians Juan Carlos Moreno García and Seth Richardson find Egyptian and Babylonian kings co-opting local powers they could not erase (6), with fields measured by many kinds of officer (16).
Munkh-Erdene, Lhamsuren
In The Nomadic Leviathan (2023), the Mongolian historian Lhamsuren Munkh-Erdene argues “for the nomadic origin of the state” in the warband of a charismatic warlord (1, 25).
Price, Richard
In First-Time (1983), the anthropologist Richard Price records how the Saramaka of Suriname keep the history of their ancestors’ war against the planters restricted, telling it only in fragments.
Querrien, Anne
Anne Querrien, the CERFI sociologist whose work on State engineering gives Deleuze and Guattari the Gothic cathedrals and the bridges of the Ponts et Chaussées (364-66).
Serres, Michel
In The Birth of Physics (1977), the philosopher Michel Serres finds a hydraulic physics in Lucretius, and with it the two sciences, "a general theory of routes and paths, and a global theory of waves" (361, 372).
Simondon, Gilbert
Following matter, as opposed to imposing form, draws on the philosopher Gilbert Simondon’s critique of the form-matter, or hylomorphic, model (408).
Will, Édouard
Édouard Will, a historian of archaic Greece, whose account of Corinth makes money derive from taxation (442).
