The five machinic processes

Five processes · front

Deleuze and Guattari “define social formations by machinic processes and not by modes of production” (435), which is to say that each column below names a process, not a kind of society. In fact, every society has all five “in perpetual interaction” (430), though usually one of them dominates and gives the society its character. They coexist “extrinsically and intrinsically” (435-37), which is to say between societies, as the brackets show, and within each one, as when warding off moves into towns (the bottom row). Each row puts the same question to all five, while the columns run from the outside, through the inside, to what crosses all of them (Axiom I, 351).

The five machinic processes, with marronage

With marronage, after Protevi · back

Protevi’s marronage column is tentative. He announces “a becoming-maroon” (RoV 96), describes marronage as “a set of interlinking processes” (108), and later treats maroon societies as a mix of prevention-anticipation, war machines, “and perhaps ecumenical” processes (119). Is marronage a process of its own, or a mix of the others? The column sits between the war machine and capture because, for Protevi, the maroon zone is “neither ‘smooth’ nor ‘striated’” (104).

Notes and sources

Five processes · notes

The markers R1-R8 on the two charts point to findings from anthropology, archaeology, and history that complicate a cell or dispute it outright. Below them is a key to every scholar and place named on the charts, including the thinkers whose concepts Deleuze and Guattari borrow (marked “cf.”), so that readers new to these literatures can follow along without a library.

Findings from the record

  1. R1 What are its poles and forms? · Warding off Ethnography complicates warding off, since among the Pintupi of Australia’s Western Desert, control of ritual knowledge founds rank as “looking after” (Myers), while in Tonga the communities that resisted their chiefs’ extraction came to stress women’s reproductive roles, which lowered women’s standing (Gailey).
  2. R2 What are its poles and forms? · War machine Can a war machine found a State? Historians of the Mongol steppe disagree. For Munkh-Erdene the warband of a charismatic warlord is the “nomadic origin of the state,” whereas for Di Cosmo steppe states formed “in relation to sedentary states” such as China, which leaves the question open.
  3. R3 How is space occupied? · War machine Anthropologists of Inner Asia have dropped “nomadism” in favor of “mobile pastoralism” (Humphrey and Sneath), much as ancient DNA studies measure migration. Yet for Deleuze and Guattari it is “false to define the nomad by movement” (381), so the specialists and the plateaus are not arguing about the same thing.
  4. R4 How is power exercised? · Polarization Archaeology reopens the question of which threshold comes first. Sumer’s city-states are ATP’s own “town solution” (432), the Indus cities had writing and standard weights but no palaces (Green), and every place name recorded in Uruk-period Mesopotamia is the name of a city (Moreno García and Richardson).
  5. R5 How is exchange made? · Capture Studies of hunter-gatherers suggest that setting food aside does not yet make a stock, which requires rights converging “upon a single interest” (Ingold). Indeed, Testart calls the order of storage and settled life “chicken-and-egg.” Both tell against the storage-first account Protevi takes up (RoV 98).
  6. R6 How is knowledge made? · Capture In ancient Egypt and Babylonia, fields were measured by a dozen kinds of officer with no fixed competence, which is to say royal measure without a royal science (Moreno García and Richardson).
  7. R7 How is violence exercised? · Capture In Middle Bronze Age Mesopotamia, violence by State and community was “rare in fact,” as people were sanctioned instead through reputation and exclusion, a regime missing from Deleuze and Guattari’s four (Garfinkle and Richardson).
  8. R8 How are persons captured or freed? · Marronage Old Babylonian law (about 1900-1600 BCE) treated a slave’s flight as theft by whoever helped, not as the fugitive’s own act (Richardson). Even whether flight counts as the fugitive’s act, then, depends on the regime.

Who and what is named

Amin, Samir
Deleuze and Guattari side with the economist Samir Amin, a theorist of world-systems and unequal development, in holding that “there can be no economic theory of international relations,” which is the ground of the ecumenical (435-36).
Bilby, Kenneth
For the anthropologist Kenneth Bilby, in True-Born Maroons (2005), the Jamaican Maroons keep a knowledge they call “Maroon Science,” learned through “testing and evasion.”
Boulez, Pierre
Smooth and striated space come from the composer Pierre Boulez, who distinguished musical time counted in advance from time occupied without being counted (362, 477-78).
Braudel, Fernand
Fernand Braudel describes the State and the town as “two runners” in the history of European capitalism, an image Deleuze and Guattari quote (434).
Carney, Judith, and Richard Rosomoff
In In the Shadow of Slavery (2009), the geographers Judith Carney and Richard Rosomoff reproduce a 1769 militia map of a runaway settlement in Minas Gerais, Brazil, marking its smithies, gardens, hidden defenses, and “holes for escape” (85).
Casimir, Jean
The Haitian historian Jean Casimir shows in The Haitians (2020) how cultivators after the revolution built a “counter-plantation” of family farms and, “without moving from the plantations,” stopped being the plantation’s workers (142), a book Protevi also cites (RoV 104).
Childe, V. Gordon
Deleuze and Guattari take the itinerant smith, who follows metal, from V. Gordon Childe, an archaeologist of prehistoric Europe who also dug in the Near East (412, 415).
Clastres, Pierre
Warding off begins with the anthropologist Pierre Clastres, whose Society against the State (1974) describes Amazonian chiefs who speak but cannot command (357-59).
Clausewitz, Carl von
The war machine’s two poles rework the distinction that the Prussian theorist Carl von Clausewitz draws between absolute war as an Idea and the real wars that States fight (420-23).
Di Cosmo, Nicola
In Ancient China and Its Enemies (2002), the historian Nicola Di Cosmo has the Xiongnu state form in reaction to China, adopting forms of sedentary states as it did so (171).
Dumézil, Georges
Sovereignty’s two heads (Varuna the binder against Mitra the jurist) come from Georges Dumézil’s comparative history of Indo-European religion (351, 424).
Gailey, Christine Ward
Christine Ward Gailey’s Kinship to Kingship (1987) treats state formation in Tonga as a process, one in which communities resisting the chiefs’ extraction came to stress women’s reproductive roles, so that women’s status fell (xi).
Garfinkle, Steven, and Seth Richardson
For Middle Bronze Age Mesopotamia (about 2000-1600 BCE), the historians Steven Garfinkle and Seth Richardson find State and community violence “rare in fact,” with sanction running instead through reputation and exclusion (161).
Glissant, Édouard
The Martinican writer Édouard Glissant, among the Caribbean writers Protevi cites, takes the maroon as a figure of resistance (RoV 121).
Gomes, Flávio dos Santos
Quilombos are Brazil’s runaway settlements. The historian Flávio dos Santos Gomes calls the networks tying them to slave quarters, the free poor, and traders a campo negro, a formation “inside slavery and an alternative to it.”
Green, Adam
In “Killing the Priest-King” (2021), the archaeologist Adam Green describes Indus cities (about 2600-1900 BCE) with writing, standard weights, seals, and metalwork, yet no palaces or royal tombs.
Humphrey, Caroline, and David Sneath
Writing on Mongolia and Inner Asia in The End of Nomadism? (1999), the anthropologists Caroline Humphrey and David Sneath call nomadism “a category imagined by outsiders” and prefer “mobile pastoralism” (1).
Husserl, Edmund
Nomad science takes its anexact rigor from Edmund Husserl’s “vague essences,” roundness as opposed to the circle (367).
Indus, Sumer, Uruk
The Indus civilization flourished in present-day Pakistan and northwest India (about 2600-1900 BCE). Sumer names the city-states of southern Mesopotamia (from about 2900 BCE), while the period of Uruk, a Mesopotamian city (about 4000-3100 BCE), gives the earliest writing, kept as accounts.
Ingold, Tim
For the anthropologist Tim Ingold, setting food aside is not yet a stock, since a stock is rights converging “upon a single interest” (1983, 553).
Jevons, W. Stanley
The limit and threshold of warding off borrow “the last” unit from the marginalist economist Jevons, whom Deleuze and Guattari dub “a kind of Lewis Carroll of economics” (437).
Lévi-Strauss, Claude
Claude Lévi-Strauss showed that the same village can present itself as segmentary and egalitarian or as encompassing and hierarchized, two potentials that Deleuze and Guattari treat as anticipations of town and State (433).
Manzanilla, Linda
The archaeologist Linda Manzanilla studies Teotihuacan, a city of central Mexico (about 100 BCE-550 CE) founded largely by people displaced by a volcanic eruption, governed corporately, and burned at its center around 550 CE.
Marx, Karl
From Karl Marx come ATP’s archaic State, his “Asiatic formation” (427), the “trinity formula” behind rent and profit as well as tax (443), and the primitive accumulation behind State violence (447).
Meillassoux, Claude
In The Anthropology of Slavery (1986), the French anthropologist Claude Meillassoux shows a West African slave system joining the societies raided for captives, the aristocracies that raid them, and the merchants who sell the captives (74).
Moreno García, Juan Carlos, and Seth Richardson
In Monarchies and the Organization of Power (2025), the historians Juan Carlos Moreno García and Seth Richardson find Egyptian and Babylonian kings co-opting local powers they could not erase (6), with fields measured by many kinds of officer (16).
Mumford, Lewis
Machinic enslavement takes its image from the historian Lewis Mumford’s “megamachine,” the archaic empire as a machine made of people (428, 457).
Munkh-Erdene, Lhamsuren
In The Nomadic Leviathan (2023), the Mongolian historian Lhamsuren Munkh-Erdene argues “for the nomadic origin of the state” in the warband of a charismatic warlord (1, 25).
Myers, Fred
Among the Pintupi of Australia’s Western Desert, as the anthropologist Fred Myers describes them in Pintupi Country, Pintupi Self (1986), authority rests in the Law, outside any living person, even as elders’ ritual knowledge founds rank as “looking after” (220-21).
Palmares
Palmares was a large runaway polity in 17th-century Brazil, which historians have described either as a kingdom or as a “republic” (RoV 118).
Price, Richard
In First-Time (1983), the anthropologist Richard Price records how the Saramaka of Suriname keep the history of their ancestors’ war against the planters restricted, telling it only in fragments.
Richardson, Seth
The historian of Mesopotamia Seth Richardson shows that Old Babylonian law treated flight as theft by whoever helped the slave (2023, 19-20).
Sibertin-Blanc, Guillaume
In State and Politics: Deleuze and Guattari on Marx (2016), the philosopher Guillaume Sibertin-Blanc treats plateaus 12-13 as a theory of violence. For him each process meets a limit of its own, so that warding off, polarization, capture, and deployment in smooth space each reach a point past which they fail (55).
Simondon, Gilbert
Following matter, as opposed to imposing form, draws on the philosopher Gilbert Simondon’s critique of the form-matter, or hylomorphic, model (408).
Testart, Alain
For the French anthropologist Alain Testart, the order of storage and settled life among hunter-gatherers is “a chicken-and-egg question” (1982, 524).
Three operations of marronage
The sheet proposes three operations of marronage, namely putting a system to flight in place, leaving it, and holding a space without leaving it.
Virilio, Paul
The “worldwide war machine” draws on Paul Virilio, a theorist of speed and war, and his “fleet in being,” which holds the sea from any point (387).
Yoffee, Norman
In Myths of the Archaic State (2005), the archaeologist Norman Yoffee treats early cities as refuges and nomadism as something urban life produces (60).
